Question 1: Impaired Mobility in Ankle Injuries — Pathomechanics

Four mechanisms that restrict movement

Ankle injuries — from an acute inversion sprain of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), through a syndesmosis disruption or bimalleolar fracture, to chronic ankle instability (CAI) — restrict movement through four interlinked pathways:

  1. Intra-articular effusion & haemarthrosis: blood and inflammatory fluid collect inside the tight talocrural joint capsule. The rising capsular tension triggers arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI) of the dorsiflexors and holds the ankle in a protective resting position of plantarflexion and inversion.
  2. Capsular pattern restriction: scar tissue, capsular fibrosis and adaptive shortening of the triceps surae produce the classic ankle capsular pattern — dorsiflexion is restricted more than plantarflexion.
  3. Loss of accessory arthrokinematics: dorsiflexion needs the talus to roll forward while gliding backward within the mortise. If fibrotic adhesions block this posterior talar glide, dorsiflexion is mechanically locked.
  4. Duality of stiffness and instability: a severe inversion sprain causes two opposite problems at once — capsular hypomobility that limits dorsiflexion, alongside ligamentous hypermobility (a torn ATFL/CFL). Unless joint position sense is retrained, the joint gives way repeatedly.

Question 3: Clinical Assessment Protocol

  • Goniometric vs weight-bearing assessment: non-weight-bearing dorsiflexion is measured with the knee flexed and then extended, to separate gastrocnemius from soleus tightness. Functional dorsiflexion is quantified with the weight-bearing knee-to-wall lunge test (normal toe-to-wall distance greater than 10 cm).
  • Posterior talar glide test: with the patient supine, the tibia is stabilised while a posterior force is applied to the anterior talus, to judge accessory joint play, stiffness and end-feel.
  • Ligamentous laxity screening: the anterior drawer test (performed at 10°-15° of plantarflexion) isolates the ATFL, while the talar tilt test (at neutral, 90°) assesses CFL laxity.
  • Sensorimotor control: single-leg stance and the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) quantify dynamic postural-stability deficits.

Question 4: Evidence-Based Mobilization Management

A. Maitland joint mobilisation system

  • Grades I & II: small- and large-amplitude oscillations performed before tissue resistance. They stimulate articular mechanoreceptors to inhibit pain through spinal gate control.
  • Grades III & IV (posterior talar glide): the clinician stabilises the posterior distal tibia and fibula while applying a posterior force over the anterior talus. Large (Grade III) or small (Grade IV) oscillations taken into end-range resistance restore posterior accessory sliding and capsular compliance.

B. Mulligan mobilisation with movement (MWM)

  • Weight-bearing dorsiflexion MWM: the patient stands with the affected foot on a chair in a lunge position. A mobilisation belt around the clinician's hips and the patient's distal leg applies a sustained anterior glide to the tibia and fibula while the clinician fixates the talus. The patient then lunges forward into pain-free dorsiflexion.
  • PILL response criteria: the movement must become Pain-free, Instantaneous and Long-Lasting. Performed as 3 sets of 10 repetitions with overpressure.
  • Fibula dorso-cranial MWM: an oblique dorso-cranial glide to the distal fibula during active inversion restores pain-free inversion in acute sprains.

Question 5: Phased Rehabilitation & Sensorimotor Progression

  • Phase 1 — Protection & oedema reduction: the POLICE protocol (Protection, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation) limits secondary hypoxic injury. Add active range of motion within pain-free limits (towel scrunches, foot alphabet).
  • Phase 2 — Mobility & muscle performance: combine Maitland Grade III-IV glides and weight-bearing MWM with progressive Achilles stretching and closed-kinetic-chain mini-squats.
  • Phase 3 — Sensorimotor & balance retraining: proprioception training on balance boards or BOSU trainers reduces the risk of recurrent sprains (Verhagen et al.). Progress to single-leg dynamic hop-and-stick landings.

Question 6: High-Yield Clinical Takeaway

Post-traumatic ankle mobility management requires treating both sides of the same problem:

  • Hypomobility — restore posterior talar glides with Maitland Grade III-IV and Mulligan MWM.
  • Instability — restore sensorimotor control and dynamic ankle stability through balance-board training.

Addressing only one side leaves the patient either stiff or unstable. The examination (posterior talar glide, anterior drawer, talar tilt, single-leg stance, SEBT) tells you which deficit dominates and therefore which to prioritise first.

Glossary of difficult terms (33 entries)

Dotted-underlined words in the text also show this definition on hover or tap.

  • Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) — The ligament at the front of the outer ankle; the structure most often torn in a lateral ankle sprain.
  • Arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI) — Reflex weakness of a muscle caused by a problem inside its joint, not by the muscle itself.
  • Arthrokinematics — The small gliding, rolling and spinning movements between joint surfaces.
  • Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) — The ligament running from the outer malleolus to the heel bone; injured in more severe lateral sprains.
  • Capsular pattern — The characteristic order in which a joint loses range when the whole joint capsule is involved. At the ankle, dorsiflexion is lost more than plantarflexion.
  • Chronic ankle instability (CAI) — Ongoing giving-way and repeated sprains after an ankle injury, due to lax ligaments and poor sensorimotor control.
  • Closed kinetic chain (CKC) — Exercises performed with the foot fixed on the ground while the body moves over it.
  • Dorsiflexion — Bending the ankle so the foot lifts upward toward the shin.
  • End-feel — The quality of resistance felt at the very end of a joint's available movement, used to judge what is limiting it.
  • Eversion — Turning the sole of the foot outward.
  • Goniometry — Measuring joint angles with a goniometer.
  • Haemarthrosis — Bleeding into a joint, which increases pressure and stiffness.
  • Hypermobility — Excessive movement at a joint, usually from lax or torn ligaments.
  • Hypomobility — Reduced movement at a joint, usually from tight or stiff tissue.
  • Inversion — Turning the sole of the foot inward; the mechanism of most ankle sprains.
  • Joint position sense — The body's ability to detect the exact position of a joint without looking at it.
  • Maitland mobilisation — Graded oscillatory passive joint movements (Grades I-V) used to reduce pain and restore movement.
  • Mobilisation with movement (MWM) — A Mulligan technique: the therapist applies a sustained accessory glide while the patient performs the painful movement.
  • Mortise — The bony socket formed by the tibia and fibula that holds the talus in the ankle joint.
  • Overpressure — Extra gentle force added at the end of a movement to gain a little more range.
  • PILL response — The Mulligan test of a correct MWM: the movement becomes Pain-free, Instantaneous and Long-Lasting.
  • Plantarflexion — Pointing the foot downward, as when standing on tiptoe.
  • POLICE — Protection, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation - the modern first-aid protocol for acute soft-tissue injury.
  • Posterior talar glide — The backward glide of the talus inside the mortise that is required for the ankle to dorsiflex.
  • Proprioception — The sense of body and joint position and movement.
  • Sensorimotor control — The combined sensory and motor processing that keeps the ankle stable during movement.
  • Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) — A dynamic balance test in which the patient reaches as far as possible in eight directions on one leg.
  • Subtalar joint — The joint between the talus and the heel bone (calcaneus) that allows inversion and eversion.
  • Syndesmosis — The fibrous joint between the tibia and fibula at the front and back of the ankle, injured in a high ankle sprain.
  • Talar tilt test — A test that stresses the calcaneofibular ligament by tilting the heel while the ankle is held at 90 degrees.
  • Talocrural joint — The main ankle joint between the talus and the tibia and fibula.
  • Triceps surae — The calf muscle group - gastrocnemius and soleus - that attaches to the Achilles tendon.
  • Weight-bearing knee-to-wall lunge test — A functional measure of dorsiflexion in which the knee is driven toward a wall with the heel down; the toe-to-wall distance is recorded.
